# Where this came from

Source notes for the report. Squash Tigers Academy is a real club; I was a
founding member and coached there from 2022 to 2026. These are my own
recollections of why the problem exists, kept here in my own words so the
report has something to draw on that is not reconstructed after the fact.

This is one practitioner's account of one program. It is domain evidence, not
research.

---

## The club outgrew the conversation

Planning started in early 2021. We opened in January 2022 — mid-season, later
than intended, because of COVID supply-chain delays — with a few kids and two
coaches.

> We were just there every day. We were doing lessons and clinics, but you kind
> of had a handle on everyone in the program and what they were working on. We
> had a good chat between the two of us — here's what this person's working on.
> Smaller groups, so more personal.

The shared record existed. It was two people talking.

What made that sufficient was that there was no gap to bridge:

> It was easy to be responsible for your kids because you were doing the
> lessons and running the squads. So you had a good handle on pretty much all
> the kids in the program between the two coaches, for a while, until it
> started to grow.

The same two people gave the lessons and ran the squads, so there was never a
question of what someone else was working on with a child. There was no someone
else.

Then it grew.

> Thankfully we were able to grow the business very quickly, scaled up pretty
> quick.

> We added a ton more kids and a few more coaches over the years, coming in and
> out.

Four coaches at Plainsboro now, clubs in Exton, Pennsylvania and Hartford,
Connecticut both hiring, and several hundred children through the door across
five years.

> As we scaled, we were just trying to keep touches on the ball and make sure — because
> it got more difficult to keep track of what people were working on. We're
> running the clinics, but you don't have a great sense of what everyone's
> working on individually.

### Two failures, not one

**Within a term**, the staff a child sees is stable. The problem is that most
of the coaches on the floor with them do not give them lessons:

> It's not that they're seeing a different coach. It's the same coaches, they
> just don't give lessons to them.

They coach a child two evenings a week in clinics with no sight of what the
lesson coach has been working on.

**Across years**, coaches come and go. When one leaves, what they knew about
thirty kids leaves with them, because it only ever existed in their head or
their own notebook.

### It is not that coaches take bad notes

> I didn't have anything super structured. For all my effort it became hard to
> keep track of things over time. I was just doing it in the Notes app on my
> phone.

A founding member, as motivated about this as anyone could be, still lost the
thread. The problem is structural rather than a matter of diligence.

## What the floor actually looks like

> The coaches are standing outside the court. There's up to like 30 kids in a
> clinic. So they're just walking up and down the courts outside. Occasionally
> they'll step in and talk to someone. But that's why it's helpful to know
> what's going on with the players — so they can see who registered before and
> take a look at those players, just so they have a general sense.

The intervention is thirty seconds through glass, perhaps twenty times in an
evening. What makes it land is knowing what that child is working on before you
open your mouth.

---

## Why it is a squash problem specifically

> A lot of squash is just individual lessons. You might have a coach keeping
> track of things in a little notebook, and then the player will come in and
> they'll look at the notebook from last time.

For one coach and one player, the notebook is enough. Most squash programs
never need more, because most are a lesson business — a coach, a court, an
hour.

> The kind of program we're running is kind of unique in the squash world.
> We're not just a lesson factory where people come in and do lessons and then
> the occasional clinic. It's really clinic-based. So it's a very scalable
> business model, which is why we're at three clubs now.

> This is actually something that lets players and coaches both see what each
> other's thoughts were on the session, but also that broader sense of it being
> a bigger program where the coaches are interfacing with more than one kid at
> a time, and kids they don't give lessons to.

> That's why this kind of app doesn't really exist for squash — because there
> aren't many programs that are as big.

The notebook model assumes the person writing and the person teaching are the
same person. In a clinic-based program they usually are not.

## Reflection

> Every time, I'd ask "what did we work on last time?" and the kid would look
> back at me with a blank stare — "oh, I don't know." So you end up doing a lot
> of the same stuff, because it's tough to build when they don't have a
> recollection of what they worked on last week. You just have the same lesson
> over and over again.

> Kids aren't reflecting and you're just doing the same thing over and over
> again, so kids will plateau. The ones who are actually able to reflect and
> keep building improve the fastest. It's more fun. And better for the kids.

And the constraint on any solution:

> As much as I harped — hey, you need to be reflecting, you need to be writing
> this down — kids don't want to do that. They just want to show up and hit a
> ball. Which is fine. But that only gets you so far.

### From the player's side

> I played squash from the time I was 9 years old. Also wasn't a big fan of
> notes, but realized as I started taking it more seriously — playing through
> the higher tiers of junior squash and college squash — that reflection was
> important. And I improved much faster when I added that element in. But I
> played professionally too.

It worked, and it took years to arrive at voluntarily, and it stayed hard to
sustain. Both halves matter: the first is why the idea is worth building, the
second is why it has to cost almost nothing to use.

### Where it sits in the literature

Ericsson's deliberate practice is defined by well-defined tasks with specific
goals, focused attention, immediate informative feedback, and repetition with
error correction. The club's drill ladder is exactly that and the coach on the
court is the feedback loop; the academy does this part well.

Reflection after the session is not one of deliberate practice's named
components. It is the third phase of Zimmerman's self-regulated learning cycle
— forethought, performance, self-reflection — which has been applied to
athletes. The academy owns the first two phases and nobody owns the third.

## Four constituencies

**The player.** They reflect, they see it written down, and they are made to
think about the session.

**The parent.** This has a number attached:

> One summer we had 50 kids at a time on 7 courts with 3 coaches in the
> building. And I had to figure out how to run that. And parents would be like,
> I feel like my kid's not getting enough individualized attention. We got too
> popular for our own good.

> Everyone likes that feel of more personalized attention, especially parents.

The attention was there. What had gone was the ability to show it.

**The coaching organisation.** Coaches can see what a colleague is working on
with the same child. The specific moment this is for is the two minutes of
group feedback between drills:

> Maybe that's where this little thing we're building can be handy — you'd know
> exactly what everyone's working on. If a lot of people are working on the
> same thing, it might help direct feedback to the group.

**Coach education and onboarding.**

> As you scale, you hire new coaches. I was onboarding coaches. It's not the
> easiest thing to do when you have competing priorities. So just give them a
> resource to look at instead of human-to-human talking. One of those things of
> being a small business with a limited staff is it's just not easy to educate
> people as quickly as you'd like. So having written stuff is good.

This is what the playbook and the methodology agent are for.

## Where the sample data comes from

> My three years of notes are what the sample players are based on. I just
> needed something to populate it. As this is built out it'll be the actual
> coaches and the actual players.

The six seeded players are invented people carrying real patterns. The cue
vocabulary, the way problems recur across months, and the gap between what a
coach writes and what a player writes are drawn from real coaching notes. The
names and every individual entry are fabricated. **No real child's record is in
this repository.**

## Next steps

> We have a registry system. Maybe we can interlink those — you have 15 kids at
> a clinic, and you can hit summarize on those 15 that have registered, get a
> readout and see if there's a lot of commonalities. Maybe you want to alter
> the session based on what kids are working on.

Summarize the room, not the player. Six kids stuck on the same rung is a
session plan, not fifteen separate notes.

Also named: wearables; squash rating as an outcome measure; building the
curriculum out further; player logins with one admin; and hosting it somewhere
so the record is genuinely shared and persists. Logins and hosting are the same
piece of work.

### A known accuracy limit

> The AI isn't perfect at figuring out whether things are routines or
> conditioned games. Long-long-short is a routine, for instance — three shots
> in sequence. Someone building out that playbook a bit more systematically
> over time might help it be a bit more accurate. But that's not a core
> feature.

A documentation gap rather than a model one: the drill taxonomy is
underspecified in the playbook, and the fix is writing it out properly with
examples.
