## 1. What the program is

A junior squash academy. Athletes are roughly 8–18 years old. The academy has three
sites, but **Plainsboro is the established club and the only one this app models** — the
other two are newly opened and have not settled into a way of running things yet.

A player's week:

| When | What | Who sets the agenda |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | **Private lesson** (1-on-1) | Coach sets the player's personal technical focus |
| Daily | **Squad** (90 min clinic) | The academy's theme of the week |
| Anytime | **Solo hitting** (24/7 court access) | Coach may assign; otherwise self-directed |
| 10–12 / year | **Tournaments** | Remote coaching, or in person at the big events |

### What each setting is for

This division is the heart of the program, and agents must not blur it.

- **The private lesson is technical.** It is where a player gets a coach's eye on grip,
  racket preparation, footwork, swing — whatever that individual is struggling with. The
  focus it sets is personal and does not depend on what week it is.
- **The squad is tactical.** It is where shots get used: when to play them, how they work
  in a match. Constraints-based drills, built around the shot of the week.
- **But the squad is also where the lesson gets rehearsed.** The blocked and routine rungs
  of a squad session are slow enough that a player can still think about their grip. That
  is where a technical focus gets its repetitions.

So the ladder carries a second meaning on top of complexity:

```
solo ──── block ──── routine ──── pattern ──── conditioned game ──── match
└──── technical rehearsal ────┘   └──────── tactical application ────────┘
          you can still think          you are making decisions
          about your own swing         and the swing has to hold up
```

A technical cue that holds at block and routine but vanishes by the conditioned game has
not been learned yet — it has only been performed. That is the single most common pattern
in this sport, and finding it is most of what this app is for.

The tension the app exists to resolve: the private lesson sets an *individual* focus,
squad runs a *shared* theme, and solo and tournaments are largely unobserved. With several
coaches, no single person sees the whole player.

### The founding observation

From the coach who designed the program, on starting a private lesson:

> Every time, I'd ask "what did we work on last time?" and the kid would look back at me
> with a blank stare — "oh, I don't know." So you end up doing a lot of the same stuff,
> because it's tough to build when they don't have a recollection of what they worked on
> last week. You just have the same lesson over and over again.

And on the obvious fix:

> As much as I harped — hey, you need to be reflecting, you need to be writing this down —
> kids don't want to do that. They just want to show up and hit a ball. Which is fine. But
> that only gets you so far.

Both halves matter. The lesson repeats because recall fails, and the standard remedy — keep
a training diary — fails because it asks more of a child than they will give. **Anything
this app asks of a player has to cost less effort than remembering would have.** That is
why capture is conversational and spoken, never a form.

## 2. How the game is played

### The governing idea

Squash is played in open space: **move the ball away from your opponent, hit it where they
are not.** Simple to say, hard to do.

From that follows the rule the whole program hangs on:

> **Long from behind. Look to take it short when in front.**

Behind your opponent, drive to the back and contain. In front of them, attack. And a
standing theme across every age group: **try to stay in front of your opponent.** Squash is
a far better game from in front.

The rule is a default, not a cage. Playing short from behind is allowed, it is just
riskier. Driving long from in front is allowed when you are unsure. **The court is never
closed down so far that it stifles a kid's creativity** — constraints bias choices, they do
not remove them.

### The T, and what pressure means

**The T** is the middle of the court, roughly equidistant from all four corners. It is what
you recover to after every shot.

Pressure has an operational definition here, and agents should use it:

> **You are under pressure when your body weight is moving away from the T as you strike
> the ball.**

That is checkable, it is what "responsible under pressure" refers to, and it is the trigger
for resetting instead of attacking.

### Tempo and accuracy

Two ways to win a rally, and a player should know which one they are using.

- **Tempo** — give the opponent less time. There are only two ways to raise it: **hit the
  ball harder**, or **take it earlier**.
- **Accuracy** — take your own time and contain. Tight straight balls, precise length.

A caution on pace from behind: banging one through occasionally is fine, but **the harder
you hit it, the faster it comes back**. The opponent reaches it earlier, and you can put
yourself under pressure with your own shot.

## 3. The shots

### Drive
To the back of the court, straight or cross.

- **B drive** (defensive / positional) — the workhorse. Floor target is **halfway between
  the back of the service box and the back wall**.
- **A drive** (attacking) — second bounce in the **back wall nick**. This is *dying
  length*: it fades into the back corner instead of coming out, and forces something loose,
  often a weak boast.
- **Cross-court from behind is the riskier one**, because it travels through the middle. It
  has to be **wide around the opponent's volley**, striking the side wall behind the back of
  the service box. More height on the front wall when in doubt.

**Front-wall targets for drives are variable** — they shift with the player, the
conditions, how loose the ball is and what the shot is for. Coaches talk about them in
the moment. Do not treat any particular front-wall height as the club's number, and do
not correct a coach's note against one. Floor targets are the stabler reference.

**The straight-drive target moves with the player's stage.** This matters — the same cue
means different things to a nine-year-old and a sixteen-year-old:

| Stage | What "good" means |
|---|---|
| Younger | Just get the depth. Far enough back is the whole job. |
| Developing | Tighter, thought about in **floorboards from the side wall** |
| Advanced | Glue it to the side wall. Contain, open the court, create the opening. |

### Boast
Side wall first. **This academy emphasises the attacking two-wall boast more than most
programs do** — kids rarely see it, so they handle it badly, which makes it a
differentiator.

- **Deception is the point.** Shape up bigger, as though driving, freeze the opponent
  behind you, then change the racket face angle so it goes side wall first and cuts in
  short.
- **Angles:** either bounce twice before the third wall, or carry a little more — a
  *working boast*, second bounce in the third-wall nick.
- **Avoid the two-and-a-half wall boast**: it bounces on the floor, kicks into the side
  wall off that bounce, pops up and hangs. That is the one that loses points.
- **Cut it**, so it stays down off the wall instead of ballooning.

### Drop
Short, into the front. Three drops are taught as distinct shots, not variations:

1. **Straight drop** — floor target about **a racket-and-a-half to two racket lengths from
   the front wall**, staying tight to the side wall.
2. **Cross-court drop into the nick** — rolls out when it lands, pops up loose when it
   misses.
3. **Cross-court drop at a narrow angle** — bounces twice before the side wall. Harder and
   more advanced, but lower risk when it works.

**Front-wall target: about a racket head above the tin.** Not an inch above — margin for
error matters more than perfection, because missing low loses the point outright.

Two ways to play a straight drop, and the choice is tactical:

- **Front wall then floor, tight to the side wall** — hangs a little longer, but tight
  means the opponent can do little with it.
- **Catch the nick** — bounces twice faster, but kicks out loose when it misses.

Use the second when the rally needs to end now; the first when staying safe matters more.

> Some players work from a front-wall target, others from a floor target. Either is fine.
> Do not correct a player onto the other one.

Also in this family: the **volley drop** (out of the air, horizontal racket shaft, extended
through) and the **counter drop** (a drop answering a drop, from the front, usually under
pressure).

### Lob
The reset shot, used **under pressure**, especially from the front of the court.

- **Cross-court**, and deliberately **not** threading the side wall. The lob is high, above
  the out line, and feathering it onto the side wall to die in the back is too hard to do
  reliably when you are already in trouble.
- **Aim for the back wall on the fly.** That guarantees the opponent is moved all the way
  back and buys time to recover to the T.
- Medium-to-small swing, hitting under the ball, follow through extended upward, posture
  low and bending from the knees.

### Kill
A hybrid, played from in front: **hit down on the ball** so it bounces twice as fast as
possible. Straight or cross.

- **Heavy slice is mandatory.** A flat ball struck with that much racket head speed just
  balloons off the walls. Cut brings it down.
- The general principle: in squash, you cut the ball on the strings.

### Racket preparation

**The single biggest technical theme in the program, across every age and level.**

> **Racket up before your feet leave the T.**

A player who runs first and prepares on arrival is already too late — the shot is chosen
for them by how late the racket is. It is also the cue most likely to look fixed in a
drill and vanish the moment a player has a decision to make, which is why it shows up so
often in this record and why it is worth tracking across weeks rather than treating each
mention as new.

Said many ways: early twitch, high neutral, hand higher on prep, racket before feet,
shoulder back early, start the swing while moving.

### Footwork — three jobs

Footwork is judged against three things, in this order:

1. **Efficiency** — get there without waste.
2. **Range of shot on arrival.** If a player can only play one shot from where they end up,
   a good opponent reads them. Arriving well is what keeps the options open.
3. **Recovery to the T.** Do not get stuck, and do not fall away from the T as you strike.

## 4. The drill ladder

**This is the spine of the program.** Every week starts at the bottom of the ladder and
climbs. Complexity increases at each rung; the shot stays the same.

| Rung | What it is | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Solo** | Player alone on court | Technique in isolation, no pressure, no decisions |
| **Block** | Two shots only — one player hits one shot, the other hits the other. Little movement. | Technique with a live ball |
| **Routine** | A set sequence of shots, in fixed order | Technique while moving, known pattern |
| **Pattern** | The same sequence with options in it | Decision-making under constraint |
| **Modified / conditioned game** | Match play with conditions attached | The shot under real pressure |
| **Match play** | Unconstrained | Whether it transfers at all |

### Routines

A fixed sequence, no choices. Named by the shots in it:

- drive, drive, drop
- drive, drive, boast
- drive, boast, cross-court lob (a Lob Week staple)
- straight drive, straight drive, cross-court drive
- long ball, long ball, short ball

### Patterns

A routine with optionality added. Named the same way — a *drive/drop pattern*, a
*drive/boast pattern*. The governing constraint is nearly always the game's own rule:

> **Must go long from behind. Look for the short ball when in front.**

Two versions of that, and the words matter because coaches use them:

- **Strict** — must go long from behind, **must** go short when in front. No choice, so
  the shot being trained gets forced. The Volley Drop Week card writes this as
  "Drive / drop - Strict".
- **Phasing** — the relaxed form. You do not *have* to go short when in front; you can
  hit deep instead. The decision comes back. `[CHECK]` term taken from a spoken
  description, not yet seen written down.

Optionality is then tuned by adding or removing constraints:

- All drives back to one side (right wall only), or to both
- Drops to one side, or to both
- An option to go short from behind as well
- A counter drop allowed in
- Dead quarters

### The quarters

The court is numbered, and coaches use these numbers constantly:

| | Front | Back |
|---|---|---|
| **Left** | **1** | **4** |
| **Right** | **2** | **3** |

A *dead quarter* is one that is out of play for that drill. Any combination can be in or
out, which is how most conditions get built.

### Conditions used at the conditioned-game rung

- Two-bounce length game
- Second ball must land behind the short line
- "Above or behind" — above the service line on the front wall, or first bounce past the
  short line
- Right-wall only / left-wall only
- Dead quarters
- Must go short when in front (forces the shot being trained)
- A volley boast option, a short-ball option, a counter allowance

**Why this matters for the app:** a player who is clean at *block* and falls apart at
*pattern* has a precisely locatable problem. The rung where a shot stops transferring is
the single most useful fact a coach can be told. Always record which rung an observation
came from.

### Session shape

Every squad session follows the same arc:

```
Warmup → Sort → [Block / Routine / Pattern / Modified Game] → Stretchdown
```

**The Sort is not a warm-up.** It is how players get matched to courts. The squad opens
with modified games — chosen to suit the week's shot, so Boast Week might open with "above
or behind" with a volley-boast option, then side versus side, then match play — played on
a **timed rotation**. Winners move up a court, losers move down. After a few rotations
everyone has settled roughly among players of their own level, having played some stronger
and some weaker on the way.

That cross-pollination is deliberate: a player sees the same shot against several
different people before the session proper begins.

**Nearly everything is scored**, including routines and patterns. Some blocked work is
not. Scoring is there for engagement as much as measurement — the academy's experience is
that juniors stay switched on when there is a score.

### The week has the same shape as the session

The ladder runs across the week as well as within a session:

```
early week   ──────────────────────────────▶   late week
block work, routines              patterns, open play, more options
most constrained                        most optional
```

A week starts tight and opens up, with how far it opens depending on the theme and where
the group is in the curriculum. So a cue failing at a pattern **on Thursday** is a
different and more advanced observation than the same cue failing in a block on Monday,
even though both are the same week.

### The bleachers moment

Coaching method is **deliberate practice**: targeted repetitions of one narrow thing,
usually shaped by a constraint rather than an instruction. The session runs in a loop:

```
drill on court  →  come off, sit on the bleachers  →  group feedback  →  back on
```

Three things govern that feedback and they make it harder than it looks:

- **It is given to the group**, not to one player. Nobody gets singled out in front of
  everyone. A coach who wants to say something personal pulls that player aside.
- **The group is mixed-level**, so one piece of feedback does not fit everyone.
- **It is sometimes split by age** — one coach takes the younger players, another the
  older ones — which means neither coach has the whole picture.

The coach has about two minutes and no notes. **This is the moment the app is most useful
to a coach:** if four of eight players on court are working on racket preparation this
week, that is what the group feedback should lead with, and right now there is no way to
know it. Surfacing the overlap across a squad — without naming anyone — turns a guess into
a decision.

## 5. The weekly themes

### The rotation

**One shot per week, in a fixed four-week cycle — drive, boast, drop, lob.** A shot is
never run two weeks running; it comes back around four weeks later and is built on from
where it was left. Three cycles make a twelve-week block, so each shot is visited three
times per block.

A non-shot theme week (see below) is slotted in when the coaches want one — commonly at the
end of a block, or in response to something they have been seeing at recent events.

This fixed period is what makes recurrence measurable. "This cue came back the last three
Drop Weeks" means twelve weeks of the same unresolved problem, not three.

### The weeks

A theme week is named for its shot. A week is built by climbing the ladder with that
shot: a graded warm-up, then blocked work, then routines, then patterns, then conditioned
games.

**The academy runs many drills for each shot, and which ones get used varies by coach,
group and level.** A few examples, to show the shape rather than to fix it:

- **Drop Week** — sort on two-bounce with a volley-drop option; routines of drive/drop and
  drive/drive/drop; modified game of long/long/short with dead quarters.
- **Lob Week** — blocked cross-court lob and boast; a drive/boast/cross-lob routine on
  diagonals, then random.
- **Boast Week** — cross-court and boast in twos or threes; drive/boast/cross routines
  with a counter allowance.

These are illustrations. **Do not treat a drill as missing because it is not on this list,
and never tell a coach a drill is not part of the program.** Each week also carries shot
targets and technique keys, which the coach running it sets.

### Non-shot themes
Occasionally a week is built around an idea rather than a shot:

- **Reset week** — height, lobs, recovering the T from under pressure
- **Positional driving week** — containing, length, keeping the opponent behind
- **Attacking week** — hitting down on the ball when in front of the opponent

## 6. The four categories

Every observation gets filed under one of four headings. The curriculum slides already
split the first two: each week lists "shot targets" (tactical) separately from "technique
keys" (technical).

| Category | What belongs here | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | How the shot is made — swing, grip, body position, footwork mechanics | "Racket head drops around at the height of the ball on the backhand" |
| **Tactical** | Shot selection, targets, court position, game plan | "Hunt the volley from the return of serve" |
| **Physical** | Movement, conditioning, mobility, injury, recovery | "Lower in the front corners — hand closer to the floor" |
| **Psychological** | Where attention is during play, scoreline management, sticking to a plan | "Thinking about where to hit it instead of my own preparation" |

Observations frequently touch two categories. File under the primary one, note the second.

### Where each one is coached

- **Technical** — the private lesson, rehearsed at the block and routine rungs of a squad.
- **Tactical** — the squad, through constraints-based drills.
- **Physical** — on-court movement and ghosting in squad; at some times of year a strength
  and conditioning coach runs separate off-court sessions. **Off-court training is out of
  scope for this app** — it records what happens on court.
- **Psychological** — mostly surfaces in the one-to-one lesson, where a player will talk
  about pressure and how they handled a match.

### What "psychological" means here — read this before tagging one

The test is **during play, not afterwards.** What was going on in the player's head while
the rally was happening. Four things live here:

**0. Emotional regulation on court.** Frustration, nerves, tilt, how a player handles a
bad call or a run of errors, whether they can reset between points. This is squarely
psychological — it is the most common thing in the category and it belongs here, not
filed away as a feeling.

**1. Where the attention is.** The academy uses Nideffer's two axes — attention has a
*width* (broad or narrow) and a *direction* (internal or external):

| | **Internal** (yourself) | **External** (the court) |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad** | Planning, running the game plan | Reading the opponent, court position |
| **Narrow** | Your own grip, prep, breathing | The ball onto the strings |

Most in-play problems are being in the **wrong quadrant at the wrong moment**, or failing
to switch between them. Maya's *"in the game I just forgot about it, I was thinking about
where to hit it"* is exactly this: she is in broad-internal, running tactics, at the
moment she needed narrow-internal, her own preparation. That is an attention problem, not
a technical one — the swing is fine, it is just not being attended to.

**2. Managing the scoreline.** Focusing at the critical points — 6-7 down, game ball.
Noticing a run going against you and stopping it. The club's rule of thumb: **do not give
up a run of three points, and do not end one with an unforced error.**

**3. Sticking to the game plan.** Having one, and not abandoning it after two lost points.

**The edge case.** An entry can be both: "I lost the prep again and got annoyed with
myself" is technical in substance and psychological in what happened next. File the
primary and note the second. If the frustration is the story, it is psychological.

Confusion about conflicting coaching is the one thing that does not belong here — that
is a signal about the coaching, and the Comparison agent is what should notice it.

### The fifth trainable

There is a fifth that the program acknowledges but does not systematically coach: **life
skills** — sleeping, eating, hydration, what a player does away from the court. It is the
largest untouched lever in junior development and the hardest to get a child to engage
with.

**Scope for this app: sleep and hydration only, plus how a player felt energy-wise.**

Food intake is deliberately excluded. Asking adolescent athletes — a population already at
elevated risk — to log what they eat, in an app their coach and parents can see, is not a
neutral feature. **No agent asks about food, weight, body composition or diet.** If a
player raises any of those unprompted, the agent does not engage with the content, does not
advise, and the entry routes to the designated safety adult under the same handling as any
other welfare signal.

Sleep and hydration are safe to ask about, easy to answer honestly, and already the two
that matter most at this age.

## 7. Time of year, age and stage

**What a player should be working on is gated by when it is and who they are.** Two cues
of equal merit are not equally appropriate; one of them may be actively wrong this month.

The hard rule, from the coach who designed the program:

> You're not going to change a kid's grip in the middle of a competitive season.

A technical rebuild costs performance before it pays it back. Starting one during a
competitive block makes a player worse at exactly the wrong time. The same change made in
the off-season is good coaching.

### The year

The junior season runs **September to March**, ending at Nationals. That shape decides
when a technical change is good coaching and when it is a mistake.

| Months | Phase | What the work is |
|---|---|---|
| **Sep – Mar** | Competitive season | Compete. Keep working at what is already in flight. **Do not start a technical rebuild.** |
| **Mar – May** | Spring, after Nationals | **The technical window.** This is when changes get made. |
| **Summer** | Off-season | Physical work, and consolidating the spring's technical changes until they hold |
| **Late summer** | Taper | Ease off, sharpen up, go into the season |

So a grip or swing rebuild flagged in November waits for **March or April**. That is not
a fudge — it is the next real opportunity, and naming the month is more useful to a coach
than "later".

**The caveat.** Tournaments run all year, and a player competing through the summer —
because of recruiting timelines or a ranking they are chasing — is on a different
calendar. Age and level change it too. If an entry suggests a player is competing out of
season, say so rather than applying the default.

**What this means for agents:**

- Before suggesting that a technical theme be escalated, consider the time of year. A
  grip or swing rebuild flagged during a competitive block should be recorded for the
  off-season, **not** pushed at the coach as urgent.
- Age and stage of development change what is reasonable to ask. An eight-year-old and a
  seventeen-year-old with the same technical fault are not given the same instruction, the
  same volume, or the same language.
- Agents surface the pattern and the timing consideration. **The coach decides.** An agent
  never rules that a change should be made now.

## 8. What a lesson note contains

A coach's own note after a private lesson, in the order it tends to be written:

| Part | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Focus** | The technical thing this lesson was about. Carries over between lessons until it is resolved. |
| **Worked on** | The activities actually run — which drills, at which rungs |
| **Going well** | What the player did better than last time |
| **Not yet** | What is still not happening, or got worse |
| **Carry forward** | What the next lesson should open with |

Kept over time, so the same focus can be followed across weeks. The note is short — it is
written between lessons, not at a desk.

## 9. Terminology

Terms coaches and players use without explanation. Agents must understand these and should
use them back.

**Court geography** — *the T* (the central recovery position) · *short line* · *service
box* · *front / mid / back third* · *quarters* (court regions, as in "dead quarters") ·
*the nick* (the join between side wall and floor) · *the tin* · *front wall target* ·
*floor target*

**Shot qualities** — *length* (how deep the ball finishes) · *width* (how far from the side
wall a cross-court travels) · *tight* (close to the side wall) · *dying length* (a ball
that expires before the back wall) · *rail* (a straight drive down the wall) · *loose* (a
ball that sits up and invites attack) · *positional* vs. *attacking*

**Technique** — *prep* (racket preparation) · *high neutral* (racket carried high between
shots) · *early twitch* (starting the racket back before moving the feet) · *swoosh* (the
acceleration point of the swing) · *hitting zone* · *point of contact* · *follow through* ·
*cut* / *slice* / *effect* · *inside cut* vs. *outside cut* · *drop around* (the racket
head dropping around the ball) · *kinetic chain* · *split step* · *last step* · *clearing*

**Tactics** — *hold* (delaying the shot to disguise it) · *delineate* (choosing
deliberately between A and B) · *show two* (presenting two shot options) · *counter* ·
*hunt the volley* · *contain* · *reset* · *guard short*

**Scoring and format** — *conditioned game* · *block* · *routine* · *pattern* · *strict* (a pattern with no choice in it) · *phasing* (a pattern with the choice put back) · *the sort* (the timed, scored rotation that matches players to courts at the start of a squad) · *dead quarter* · *quarters 1–4* (front left, front right, back right, back left) ·
*constraints-based* (the academy's name for its drill design: rather than telling a player
what to do, the drill's rules make the right choice the available one)

## 10. Cue library — the chronic ones

These cues recur across coaches, players and years. When one appears in an entry, tag it to
the canonical cue below rather than treating it as new. **A cue that keeps reappearing for
the same player is not a reminder — it is an unresolved blocker, and should be escalated.**

| Canonical cue | Category | Also said as |
|---|---|---|
| Racket before feet | Technical | early twitch · high neutral · racket up before you move · hand higher on prep |
| Step then swing | Technical | sequence · last down first up · plant then hit · time the last step |
| Space next to the ball | Technical | don't crowd · more side of the ball · contact further back in the stance · don't jam the arm |
| Left arm engaged | Technical | left arm both sides · counterbalance on the backhand · left arm out of the way on the forehand |
| Squeeze the grip | Technical | stronger racket in hand · don't go floppy · feel the bottom fingers |
| Follow through to a target | Technical | don't stop the swing · extend to the corner · loop for power |
| Put effect on the ball | Technical | cut · slice · inside/outside cut · don't be too flat |
| Hunt the volley | Tactical | take it early · step forward, don't run back · guard short |
| Aim the drive | Tactical | delineate A and B · front wall target · floor target · tight |
| Get lower | Physical | soften the knees · hand closer to the floor · bend from the knees |
| Watch them hit | Tactical | don't move early · anticipate · split step on the T |
| Responsible under pressure | Psychological | reset · don't force it · take the height |

## 11. Safeguarding — what this app is not

**This is a coaching record, not a communication platform.** Everyone using it is working
with children, and that constrains the design more than any other requirement here.

### Deliberate non-features

These are not missing. They were considered and refused.

- **No messaging between coaches and players.** No inbox, no reply threads, no direct
  messages. A coach does not "send" a player anything; they write a training note, and the
  player reads it.
- **No notifications that initiate contact with a child.** Nudges appear on the player's
  own screen when they open the app. Nothing reaches out to them.
- **No private channel of any kind between an adult and a child.**

The reason: private, unobservable one-to-one contact between an adult and a minor athlete
is the central safeguarding risk in youth sport, and the standard across the sport is that
contact should be sport-related and open to view. An app that quietly becomes a messaging
layer has created the exact risk every club policy exists to prevent.

### Observable by default

**The parent's read-only view is the safeguarding control, not a convenience.** Everything
a player receives from a coach is attached to a training record and visible to that
player's guardian. That is what makes coach-to-player contact here legitimate.

The two private tiers are not exceptions to this, because neither is a message to a child:

- **A coach's private note** is a professional record for other coaches. It is not
  addressed to the player, so it is not adult-to-child contact.
- **A player's private entry** is the player's own reflection space. It is private *from
  coaches*. It is **not** private from safeguarding: a welfare signal in a private entry
  routes to the designated safety adult and to the player's parent, and **the player is
  told that this has happened.** They are never deceived about who can see what.

### Rules for every agent

- **Never relay.** An agent points a player at what a coach wrote, or tells them to ask
  their coach. It never carries a message from one person to another in its own words,
  and it never acts as a go-between.
- **Never initiate.** No agent starts a conversation with a child.
- **Never promise confidentiality it cannot keep.** If a player says "don't tell anyone"
  or "keep this between us", the agent does not agree. It says plainly what is private
  from coaches and what is not, and it still escalates a welfare signal.
- **Never coach.** Squash advice comes from a coach, not from an agent. This is a
  safeguarding rule as much as a quality one: an agent that gives instruction has become
  an unsupervised adult voice in a child's training.

## 12. How agents should talk

- **To players:** plain language, matched to age. Encouraging. Short. Ask at most one or
  two follow-up questions. Use the club's own words for shots and drills.
- **To coaches:** terse and specific. Lead with the rung and the shot. No motivational
  filler.
- **To parents:** plain English, no jargon, no diagnosis of the child.

**Agents never give squash coaching advice of their own.** They organize, connect and
surface what coaches have said. When a player asks "how do I fix my backhand," the answer
points at the relevant coach note, or says to ask the coach — it does not invent technique.
